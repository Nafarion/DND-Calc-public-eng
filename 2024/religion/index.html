Faith in the worlds of D&D

Religion is an important part of life in the D&D multiverse. When gods walk the world, clerics work miracles and paladins stand against darkness, it is hard to doubt that the gods exist.

Many people pray to different gods depending on the circumstances: in the Forgotten Realms the same person might ask Sune for luck in love, make an offering to Waukeen before market day and appease Talos in a storm on the same day. Many have a favorite deity whose ideals they share, and a few devote themselves entirely to one god — usually as its priests or champions.

Every world has its own pantheons. The gods of nonhuman peoples — Moradin of the dwarves, Corellon Larethian of the elves — are worshiped on many worlds, though in different ways.

Choosing a god for your character

Your DM decides which gods exist in the campaign. From them you can choose one your character serves, worships or pays lip service to; several they turn to most often; or simply remember who is revered in the world to mention them when it fits.

A cleric and a character with the Acolyte background should decide which god they serve or served and look at the deity’s suggested domains.

Clerics

A cleric is an intermediary between the mortal world and the planes of the gods. Their magic comes from a deity, a pantheon or another immortal entity: a cleric draws on the divine power of the Outer Planes where the gods dwell, and is usually tied to a temple of their deity or other power.

A cleric’s subclass is a domain — a sphere of existence revered by a god, a pantheon or a religious order. For example, the Life Domain is sought not only by clerics of gods but also by religious orders of healers.

Paladins

A paladin’s power lies in their oath. An oath can be sworn before a god’s altar, in a sacred grove before nature spirits, or in a moment of desperation with only the dead as witnesses. Many paladins of the Oath of Devotion serve gods of law and good; among the gods paladins revere are Torm, Tyr, Heironeous, Paladine, Kiri-Jolith, Dol Arrah, the Silver Flame, Bahamut, Athena, Re-Horakhty and Heimdall.

Druids

A druid draws power from nature itself, from a nature deity, or from both. Many druids seek a mystical communion with nature rather than service to a deity. In Greyhawk the druidic faith is called the Old Faith: it reveres nature as a primal force, along with Beory, Obad-Hai, Ehlonna and Ulaa. In the Forgotten Realms a druid circle usually isn’t tied to one god: druids revere Silvanus, Mielikki, Eldath, Chauntea and even the harsh Gods of Fury — Talos, Malar, Auril and Umberlee.

Monks

Some monastic traditions are tied to gods who value physical perfection and discipline of the mind. In the Forgotten Realms the Order of the Dark Moon serves Shar, and the monasteries of Ilmater are named after flowers. Monasteries in Eberron are mostly devoted to the gods of the Sovereign Host, monks in Dragonlance most often serve Majere, and in Greyhawk — Xan Yae and Zuoken.

Warlocks: a patron is not a god

Sometimes a warlock relates to their patron as a cleric does to a deity, but warlock patrons aren’t gods, though they are nearly as powerful. A warlock might lead a cult of a demon prince, an archdevil or an alien entity — beings clerics usually don’t serve. More often their relationship is like that of a master and an apprentice. About fiend patrons: Archdevils and demon lords.

Not every priest is a cleric

Not every temple servant is a cleric with divine magic. Some priests simply serve their god with prayer and rites, and in some cities a priestly rank is just a political post with no connection to a deity. True clerics are rare; the god usually sends them on adventures personally.
